Structural Drying and Dehumidification in Melrose, MA

Moisture meters, air movers and dehumidifiers work until the building itself reads dry. The 24/7 line is open if water is still spreading.

Call (339) 333-9345

Structural drying is the part of a water loss that happens after the visible water is gone. Wood framing, subfloors, drywall and plaster hold moisture long after a floor looks dry, and structural drying uses meters, air movers and dehumidifiers to pull that water out in a controlled way. In Melrose, most calls come after a pipe failure, a roof leak or a basement seep.

If water is still moving through the house, call the 24/7 emergency line first and shut off the source. Drying only works once the leak has stopped. Same-day response is offered when a crew is available, and the scope and price are explained before work starts.

The floor looks dry but the room still feels damp. Is it dry?

Not necessarily. A surface can feel dry to the hand while the stud bottoms, the subfloor and the back of the drywall are still wet. That is why a crew reads the materials with meters instead of trusting touch or smell.

Signs that moisture is still inside include a musty smell that returns, baseboards that stay cool, paint that keeps bubbling, and floor boards that keep cupping. Any of these means the building is not dry yet, even if the puddle is long gone.

The drying window and why the clock matters

The Massachusetts Department of Public Health homeowner guide on mold says wet areas should be dried completely within 24 hours. It also notes that the state Housing Code expects areas exposed to leaks and floods to be dried within 48 hours.

After a flood or a long-running leak, that window can be hard or impossible to meet, which is why equipment goes in as early as possible. The same guide points out that mold can grow in hidden places such as inside walls and under carpet, so dry means dry all the way through.

How a drying job runs, day by day

  1. Map the moistureA crew walks every room with a pin or pinless meter and a thermal camera, then marks the wet zones on a simple sketch.
  2. Set a drying goalReadings in an unaffected area of the same material become the target, so each material has its own finish line.
  3. Place the equipmentAir movers aim along walls and under cabinets while dehumidifiers pull water vapor out of the air. Doors, closets and wall cavities are opened as needed.
  4. Check and adjustThe crew returns each day to read the same spots, move equipment and add or remove units as the readings fall.
  5. Confirm dry and remove equipmentEquipment leaves only when every marked spot reaches its goal, and the final readings go into your record.

Why dehumidifiers matter as much as fans

Air movers make water leave the material, but that water has to go somewhere. If it stays in the room as vapor, it settles back into drywall and wood. Dehumidifiers collect that vapor and drain it away, which is why a fan alone rarely finishes a job.

The published industry standard for water damage restoration is ANSI/IICRC S500, and it treats drying as a measured process with a clear goal. You can ask any company whether its crews work to it and whether they log readings each day.

How many days will it take?

Drying often takes several days, and the real number depends on the materials, how wet they got and how soon equipment started. Thin drywall may dry quickly, while thick framing, hardwood and concrete can take longer. We cannot promise a date, but the daily readings show the trend.

If a reading stalls, the crew adjusts airflow or opens a cavity instead of leaving equipment running blindly. A job ends when measurements say it should, not when a calendar does.

Electrical, noise and what to expect at home

Drying equipment draws electrical power, and a crew spreads the load across circuits so a breaker does not trip while you are away. Air movers and dehumidifiers are loud, and most households find the noise noticeable for a few days. Warm air from the units can raise the temperature in a closed room.

If water has reached an outlet, a cord or the panel, stay clear and call the utility or 911 before you call us. Equipment is never plugged into a wet circuit.

Safety steps while drying is under way

Do not turn off the equipment to save noise, and do not move it because it blocks a hallway. Moving a unit can leave a wet pocket behind. Keep children and pets out of rooms with cords across the floor, and keep the exterior doors to the rest of the house closed so humidity does not spread.

Older Melrose houses and hidden moisture

Melrose grew as a commuter suburb once rail stops opened by 1845, so many houses are older and may have plaster over lath or tall, open wall cavities. In general, older framing and plaster can hide water longer than new drywall, which makes mapping before patching especially useful.

Neighboring towns share the same kinds of buildings. Our crews dry homes near the Mystic River in Medford and low-lying areas near the Saugus River in Saugus using the same process.

Mistakes that slow drying

The most common mistakes are closing windows and doors in a wet house, running a household fan with no dehumidifier, and patching a wall before the cavity is dry. Another is turning heat up so high that vapor condenses on a cold exterior wall. Each one leaves moisture behind to feed mold later.

EPA guidance says to fix plumbing leaks and other water problems as soon as possible and to dry hard surfaces completely. A drying plan that skips either step is only half a plan.

Insurance and your drying record

Sudden and accidental water damage is often covered by a homeowners policy, while gradual leaks and neglect often are not, and flood damage usually needs separate flood coverage. Check your policy or ask your insurer. A daily log of readings, photos and equipment placement gives you a clear record to share.
