The published ANSI/IICRC S500 standard covers water damage restoration up to the point where repair begins.

Repair starts only when the structure is dry

A fresh coat of paint over damp drywall looks fine for a week and then peels. Before anything is rebuilt, moisture meters confirm that framing, subfloors and the back of the wall are at normal readings. If they are not, drying continues first, which is the job of water mitigation.

The Massachusetts Department of Public Health guide for homeowners also says porous materials contaminated by mold, sewage or flood water, including wallboard, trim and insulation, should be discarded. Repair planning begins with that list of what must come out.

Ceiling and wall water damage repair

A stain on a ceiling or a soft spot in a wall usually points to water that arrived from above or behind. Repair begins by cutting out the wet or sagging section back to solid framing, checking the insulation behind it, and replacing anything that stayed wet. Small stains on a dry ceiling may need only stain-blocking primer and paint.

Larger sections are replaced with new drywall, taped, finished and primed with a stain-blocking primer so the old mark does not bleed through. Plaster ceilings are repaired differently, using patching plaster or a skim coat.

If a hole is forming or the ceiling is bulging with water, treat it as unsafe. Stay out from under it, and call. The weight of trapped water can bring a section down without warning. Wall damage from a siding or window leak follows the same sequence, with the exterior leak fixed first.

Hardwood floors: cupping, drying and what can be repaired

Hardwood cups when the underside of the boards takes on more moisture than the top. The edges rise and the middle sits low, a pattern common after a washer valve failure, a dishwasher leak or an air conditioner condensate line that backs up. Boards that cup can often flatten after a controlled drying period, so sanding too early can leave you with a dished floor once the wood settles.

Floor drying systems pull moisture out of the boards and the subfloor with air flow through mats or panels. When boards stay cupped, are loose from the subfloor or show black staining, they are replaced and the surrounding floor is blended in. Walnut, oak and maple all react the same way to water, though finishes and board widths change how the repair looks.

  3. Replace insulation and boardNew insulation and drywall go in, taped and finished to match the surrounding surface.
  4. Prime with stain blockerA stain-blocking primer seals old marks so they stay hidden.
  5. Paint and flooringPaint, trim and flooring finish the room. Hardwood is sanded and refinished after it settles.

Permits and older-home rules in Melrose

The Melrose Department of Inspectional Services requires permits under the state building code to repair, renovate or install new material, with separate permits for plumbing, electrical, gas and HVAC work. Ordinary repairs are exempt, as are carpeting, painting and wallpapering. Ask the department about your specific job before you start, because applications go through the city's online permitting portal.

Older homes bring another rule. Work that disturbs more than 6 square feet of interior painted surface must follow the Massachusetts lead renovation rule, 454 CMR 22.11, which matters in older houses. Ask any contractor how they handle it before demolition.

Repair and your insurer

Sudden and accidental water damage is often covered by a homeowners policy, while gradual leaks and neglect often are not, and flood damage needs separate coverage. Ask your insurer which category your loss falls into. Keep photographs of the damage before repair and a written scope so you can compare it with the adjuster's.
