My wall is wet and the carpet is soaked. What is water mitigation?

It is the stop-the-damage stage that comes before any rebuilding. A wet wall, a squishy carpet or a stain that keeps spreading all mean water is still working into the building, and mitigation is the trade name for pulling that water out and drying the structure. The sooner it starts, the more of the drywall, flooring and framing can stay in the house.

When standing water is an emergency

Treat it as an emergency when water is still running, when it covers more than a small patch of floor, or when it has reached insulation, subfloors or anything electrical. Shut off the main valve, then call. If the water is near the panel, an outlet or a gas appliance, stay out and call the utility or 911 about any electrical or gas risk before you call us.

Water from a clean supply line is different from water that came up through a floor drain or in from outdoors. Anything that touched sewage or ground runoff is handled as contaminated, which changes what can be dried in place. Our dispatcher asks about the source on the first call.

What the first 24 to 72 hours look like

  1. Find and stop the sourceThe crew confirms the leak is stopped or the main is off, because drying a room with an active leak never ends.
  2. Map the moistureMoisture meters and a thermal camera show how far the water traveled behind baseboards, under flooring and inside wall cavities, so equipment goes where the water actually is.
  3. Remove standing waterPumps and extraction units pull water off floors and out of carpet, which is the fastest way to cut drying time.
  4. Set the drying equipmentAir movers push dry air across wet surfaces while dehumidifiers pull the moisture out of the air. Placement is adjusted as the readings change.
  5. Monitor until dryReadings are logged each visit and equipment comes out only when the materials reach their dry standard.

Structural drying: how wet framing and subfloors get dry

Structural drying is the part of mitigation that deals with the building itself rather than the contents. Water wicks upward into drywall, soaks into stud bottoms and sits in the gap between a subfloor and finished flooring. A crew drills small relief holes, lifts baseboards or pulls back a corner of carpet so air can reach the wet cavity.

The Massachusetts Department of Public Health mold guide says wet areas should be completely dried within 24 hours and notes that the state Housing Code expects areas exposed to leaks and floods to be dried within 48 hours. After a flood it can be hard or impossible to hit that window, which is why starting equipment the same day matters. Readings, not guesses, decide when drying is finished.

Material that cannot be dried in time, such as soaked insulation or wallboard that has been under water, is cut out and bagged. The crew keeps that cut as small as the readings allow.

Insurance and documentation

Sudden and accidental water damage is often covered by a homeowners policy, while slow leaks and neglect often are not, and flood damage usually needs separate flood coverage. Check your policy or ask your insurer before assuming either way. We photograph the wet areas, log moisture readings and note equipment placement so you have a clear record to share.

Where mitigation ends and the next steps begin

Mitigation gets the building dry and stable. If the water came from a burst pipe or a failed appliance, emergency water removal covers the first pumping and extraction. Once the structure is dry, water damage cleanup handles sanitizing and debris, and mold remediation applies if growth has already started. Rebuilding comes last.
