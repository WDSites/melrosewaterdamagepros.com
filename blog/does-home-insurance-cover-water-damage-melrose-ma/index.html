Does Home Insurance Cover Water Damage? A Melrose, MA Guide

By the Melrose Water Damage Pros team. Published and updated October 29, 2026.

Sometimes yes, sometimes no, and the dividing line is the cause of the water. A standard homeowners policy often responds to water damage that is sudden and accidental, such as a pipe that bursts or an appliance hose that fails. It usually does not respond to flood damage, and it may not respond to a slow leak that went unfixed for months.

This is general information, not a coverage opinion. Policies differ, so the only answer that counts for your house comes from your own policy documents and your insurer. What follows will help you ask better questions and avoid mistakes that weaken a claim.

Sudden and accidental versus gradual

Massachusetts Division of Insurance guidance describes water damage from non-flood causes, such as a burst pipe, as usually covered when it is sudden and accidental, though the insurer will investigate the cause. The wording matters because a pipe that fails overnight and a joint that has dripped behind a wall for a year are treated differently.

Gradual leaks, long-term seepage, and damage from neglect are the situations policies commonly exclude. If you spot a slow leak, fixing it promptly and keeping records of when you noticed it protects your position.

Flood and backup: the two big gaps

The Division of Insurance states that standard homeowners policies do not cover flood damage, even for flooding away from oceans and rivers. A basement that takes on water from heavy rain or rising groundwater generally falls on the flood side of the line.

Some coverage may exist if your policy includes an endorsement, also called a rider, for sump pump failure or water backup. Look at the declarations pages or ask your agent. Many homeowners learn about this gap only after a loss, when it is too late to add it.

How to document water damage for a claim

The state's guidance on documenting a loss is straightforward, and following it while the damage is fresh makes the claim easier to review.

  • Photograph the outside of the property and any visible flooding or damage.
  • Photograph the inside, including damaged belongings and the height the water reached on walls.
  • Separate damaged items from undamaged ones and set them aside for the adjuster to review.
  • Protect the property from further damage where reasonably possible, such as shutting off the water or removing standing water.
  • Use your home inventory when listing what was lost.
  • Keep records of moisture readings, drying logs, and invoices from the restoration company.

Why mitigation steps matter for coverage

Insurers generally expect you to take reasonable steps to limit damage after a loss. Waiting days to remove standing water can allow mold and further material damage, and that can complicate a claim.

This is where prompt water damage cleanup helps. Our crews record moisture readings and what was removed, so you have a clear record to share with your insurer. We do not decide coverage, and we cannot tell you what your policy will pay.

Questions worth asking before you need to

Read your policy once when nothing is wrong. Look at the deductible, the definition of water damage, any exclusions for gradual loss, and whether you have a backup endorsement. If you live in a low-lying area, ask about flood insurance as well.

Flood claims and the waiting period

If you carry a National Flood Insurance Program policy, the state notes there is usually a 30-day waiting period before coverage takes effect. The exception is a policy bought while a loan closes, which has no waiting period.

That means flood coverage cannot be bought on the day a storm is forecast. For standing water after flooding, our flood damage restoration page explains how crews handle contaminated water.

Local note

Melrose notes: what the Division of Insurance says

The Massachusetts Division of Insurance reports that 48.6 percent of the state's homeowners' claims in 2024 were for non-flood water losses and freezing damage. Water claims are the common claim here, not the rare one, which is a good reason to understand your policy now.

For burst pipes, the Division notes that coverage generally depends on the home being properly heated and not left unoccupied. Melrose homeowners who travel in winter should keep heat running and have someone check the house.

The Division also runs a Consumer Services Unit for insurance questions. If you are unsure how your policy words a water loss, that unit is a neutral place to ask, separate from your own insurer.
