Basement Mold Removal Cost in Melrose, MA: What Sets the Price

By the Melrose Water Damage Pros team. Published and updated October 29, 2026.

Basement mold removal in Melrose costs whatever the scope of the work adds up to, and the scope depends on a short list of factors you can see for yourself. How much surface is affected, what the growth is on, how much has to be sealed off, and how long drying takes do most of the work. This page does not print a price because a number without a scope would mislead you.

What you can do is learn how a fair estimate is built. Once you know the factors, a written scope becomes easy to read, and a vague one becomes easy to spot.

Why one number cannot fit every basement

Two basements in the same neighborhood can need completely different work. One has a damp patch on painted block behind a water heater. The other has wet drywall, soaked insulation, and a finished floor that was never dried after a pipe failed.

A company that quotes both over the phone for the same amount is guessing at one of them. Price follows labor, materials, equipment, and days on site, and each of those changes with the damage.

A written estimate should name each of these factors for your basement rather than offering one lump figure.

How scope is actually set

A scope starts with a walk-through. The inspector looks at visible growth, probes suspect walls, reads moisture with a meter, and traces the water back to its source. Those readings are what separate a surface problem from one that runs into the structure.

From there the scope lists what is cleaned, what is removed, how the work area is sealed, which equipment runs, and what the area looks like when the crew leaves. When you request an estimate, scope and price are explained before work starts, so you can compare line by line.

Why testing does not set the price

Many homeowners assume a lab test determines the cost. The Massachusetts Department of Public Health homeowner guide says there are no federal or state standards defining a safe amount of mold, and it does not recommend air or bulk sample testing.

It adds that testing does not change the requirement to clean visible mold and fix the moisture source. In plain terms, a lab report rarely changes what the crew does, so the price follows the damage you can see and measure rather than a test result.

There is one exception worth knowing. If a lender, buyer, or landlord asks for a separate clearance check after the work, that is an independent step with its own cost, and it is worth asking in advance what standard the checker will use.

What to do before you call about basement mold

Do not disturb a patch more than you must. Before you collect quotes, protect the space and the record.

  • Do not move moldy boxes through the house, because that spreads growth upstairs.
  • Keep the dehumidifier running and the basement door closed while you wait.
  • Note where water enters after storms, since that source drives the scope.
  • Photograph each wall and keep any moisture readings the visitor gives you.

Questions that make an estimate comparable

Ask each company the same set of questions, and keep the answers in writing.

  • What square footage and which materials does the scope cover, and where was it measured?
  • Which materials are being removed, and which are being cleaned?
  • How will the work area be sealed, and what air filtration runs during the job?
  • How many drying days are planned, and what readings tell the crew it is done?
  • Does the estimate include rebuild, or does it stop once removal and drying are finished?
  • Do the crews work to the ANSI/IICRC S520 mold standard, and what does the final check look like?

Costs that sit next to mold removal

Removal and cleaning is one line. The source of the water is another, and it is the one people forget. If a pipe, window well, or drain caused the moisture, fixing it is separate from remediation, and skipping it means the money was spent twice.

When water is still present, water mitigation is usually the first move, since extraction and drying stop the damage from growing. After that, mold remediation handles the growth and the affected materials, and a rebuild follows if drywall or flooring had to come out.

Insurance, in general terms

Sudden and accidental water damage is often covered under a homeowners policy, while gradual leaks and neglect often are not. Read your policy or ask your insurer how mold that follows a covered event is treated. Nothing here predicts what a claim will pay.

Local note

Melrose notes: permits and what a scope should include

Melrose permits come from the Department of Inspectional Services, also called the Melrose Building Department. The state building code requires permits to repair, renovate, or install new material, and the city lists separate permits for plumbing, electrical, gas, and HVAC work. Ask whether the rebuild portion of a basement job triggers one.

Ordinary repairs are exempt, and so are carpeting, painting, and wallpapering. That is useful when you read an estimate: the paint and carpet lines need no city permit, while moving a pipe or rewiring a panel does. Applications go through the city's online permitting portal, so build that lead time into your timeline.

The state guide also tells homeowners to hire professionals for plumbing, electrical, and structural work, which may legally require a licensed contractor. If a mold estimate quietly includes that work, ask who performs it and who pulls the permit.
