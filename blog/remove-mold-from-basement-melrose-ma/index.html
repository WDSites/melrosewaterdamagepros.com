How to Remove Mold From a Basement, Surface by Surface, in Melrose, MA

By the Melrose Water Damage Pros team. Published and updated October 29, 2026.

The right way to remove mold from a basement depends on what the mold is growing on. Hard, non-porous surfaces such as poured concrete and sealed block can usually be cleaned. Soft, absorbent materials such as drywall, insulation, carpet, and cardboard often cannot be cleaned and dried well enough, and they are the ones that end up in the trash.

Below, a Melrose basement is walked through one surface at a time, with the table showing what is cleaned and what is discarded. Everything in the discard list comes from the Massachusetts Department of Public Health homeowner guide.

Start with the moisture, not the mold

Mold shows up where moisture lingers, so the first job is to name the source. The state guide splits sources into two groups. Acute ones are one-time events such as an overflowing tub, an appliance leak, a burst pipe, a sewer backup, or storm flooding.

Chronic ones are the slow kind: leaking faucets, small leaks inside walls or under cabinets, clogged gutters, damaged roofing or flashing, failed caulking, condensation on uninsulated cold-water pipes, and humidity that never gets controlled. In a basement, the quiet chronic sources are the ones people miss.

A crew decides final calls in the field with moisture readings, because two pieces of the same material can be in different condition.

Concrete, block, and framing

Poured concrete and block look solid, but block is hollow and the wall behind your paint may be damp. Scrub the visible growth with detergent and water, rinse with a clean wet cloth, and run a dehumidifier until the wall feels dry. If the same band of block darkens again within a week, moisture is moving through the wall.

Framing lumber needs a closer look. Surface growth on solid wood is a cleaning job, but a stud or sill that feels spongy or splits under a screwdriver tip is no longer a homeowner decision. The state guide treats structural work as a job for professionals.

Drywall, insulation, and carpet

The state guide names gypsum wallboard, wooden trim and molding, and insulation as porous materials that should be discarded when contaminated with mold, sewage, or flood water. Carpet and ceiling tiles go too if they cannot be cleaned and dried effectively.

Cutting out wet wallboard is not complicated, but it is dusty. Cut back to clean, dry material, bag the debris on the spot, and keep the work area closed off. Do not dry scrape, and do not paint or caulk over what remains.

If the removal runs beyond a few square feet, or it touches wiring, plumbing, or the structure, mold remediation is the safer route. EPA guidance puts professional remediation at areas over about 10 square feet.

Stored boxes and soft goods

Basements collect cardboard, and cardboard soaks up moisture like a sponge. Moldy boxes should be discarded, and what was inside can be wiped or washed if it is hard or washable. Move everything you keep into sealed plastic bins and raise them off the floor.

Upholstered furniture, mattresses, and stuffed items are harder. If they were wet and now show growth, they are usually discarded. Sentimental pieces are worth a call to a restoration company before you decide, though no one can promise a rescue.

Drying is the step that lasts

Whatever you clean, dry it. The state guide says wet areas should be completely dried in 24 hours, and that flood-related damage can make that window difficult or impossible, which is how growth takes hold.

When the basement is damp across the board, water mitigation brings in air movers and dehumidifiers and takes readings until the materials are dry. Neighbors in Wakefield and Malden rely on the same process when a wet basement turns into a mold problem.

Local note

Melrose notes: city water and basement dampness

Melrose buys its water from the MWRA, which delivers it from transmission mains into the city's distribution system of about 90 miles of pipe. The city replaces leak-prone pipes and services and runs annual leak detection. A basement that stays wet with no plumbing source on your side may deserve a call to the city to ask whether a leak is outside your walls.

Before you pay for cleaning, run a simple test. Shut off every water use in the house, check the meter, and watch whether it keeps moving. A moving meter points to a leak you can fix, and fixing it comes before any mold work.

The state guide's list of chronic sources applies in Melrose basements as much as anywhere. Check gutters and downspouts, look for condensation on cold-water pipes, and watch the humidity near the laundry area, since uncontrolled humidity in basements and laundry rooms is on the guide's list.
