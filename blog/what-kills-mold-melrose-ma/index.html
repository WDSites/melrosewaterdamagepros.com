What Kills Mold? An Honest Answer for Melrose, MA Homeowners

By the Melrose Water Damage Pros team. Published and updated October 29, 2026.

No single spray, fog, or paint kills mold for good, and anyone selling that promise is skipping the part that matters. Mold grows where moisture stays, so the working answer is a three-part routine: physically clean it off, dry the surface completely, and remove the water source. For hard surfaces, the Massachusetts Department of Public Health says detergent and water generally does the cleaning.

The rest of this page sorts the common products and habits by what the state guide and EPA actually say about them, and it points out when the job is bigger than a bucket and a brush.

Killing versus removing

Many products are sold on the idea of killing mold, but the practical goal is to remove it. Dead mold material left on a surface is still material on the surface, and it still needs to be cleaned off. Scrubbing with detergent and water lifts it, and drying prevents the next round.

That is why the state guide focuses on cleaning and drying rather than on chemical treatments. It says mold can generally be cleaned with detergent and water, and that harsh chemicals like bleach or biocides are usually not recommended.

Why painting over mold fails

Paint is sold in some stores as mold resistant, and it is tempting to treat a stain with a fresh coat. Painting a damp surface traps moisture beneath the coating. The mold material is still there, and the water is still there, so the stain reappears through the paint.

The state guide is blunt about this: do not paint or caulk over mold. Clean first, dry fully, fix the leak, and only then refinish the surface.

Moisture control is the real treatment

Mold needs moisture, so removing the moisture is what stops new growth. The guide separates acute sources, such as burst pipes, appliance leaks, sewer backups, and storm flooding, from chronic ones, such as slow leaks inside walls, leaking faucets, clogged gutters, failed caulking, and condensation on cold-water pipes.

Uncontrolled humidity in basements, bathrooms, laundry rooms, and kitchens is on that list too. Dehumidifiers and exhaust fans are the guide's recommended tools. If a plumbing leak or a wet event started the problem, water mitigation removes standing water and dries the structure with monitored equipment.

When a crew is the right answer

EPA guidance says a moldy area under about 10 square feet can usually be handled by the household, and larger areas or heavy water damage call for professional remediation. The state guide also says porous materials such as wallboard, insulation, and trim should be discarded when contaminated.

Mold remediation puts containment, filtered air, removal, and drying in a set order under the ANSI/IICRC S520 standard. You can ask any company whether its crews follow it. Customers in Saugus and Medford get the same process as those in Melrose.

About the word permanently

You may see products that promise to kill mold permanently. No honest company can promise that, because moisture can return at any time. A better test of any plan is whether it names the moisture source, the drying target, and the cleaning method.

Local note

Melrose notes: older houses, renters, and the Housing Code

Melrose grew as a commuter suburb after Boston and Maine Railroad stops opened by 1845 at what are now Wyoming Hill, Cedar Park, and Melrose Highlands. Older homes often have older plumbing and old paint, so a leak can run inside a wall for a long time. A slow leak is a chronic source on the state guide's list, and it explains why mold sometimes appears with no obvious wet event.

The state guide notes that work disturbing more than 6 square feet of interior painted surface must follow lead renovation rules, 454 CMR 22.11. In an older Melrose house, that is one more reason to prefer wet cleaning over sanding or scraping.

The guide also refers to a Housing Code requirement to dry areas exposed to leaks and floods within 48 hours. If you rent in Melrose and a leak has gone unrepaired, read that section of the guide with your landlord. It is also a useful rule of thumb for owners.

Frequently asked questions

Neither the state guide nor the EPA page covered here recommends vinegar. Their guidance is detergent and water, scrubbing hard surfaces, and drying completely. What matters is removing the growth and the moisture, not the choice of household cleaner.

The state guide says it is usually not recommended. It also gives a limit if you do use it, no more than 1 cup of laundry bleach per gallon of water, and warns never to mix bleach with ammonia-based products.

Fix the water source, dry the area completely, and control humidity with dehumidifiers and exhaust fans. Mold returns when moisture does, so no cleaning step stands in for the repair.

The state guide does not recommend air or bulk testing, and it says no federal or state standards define a safe amount of mold. Testing does not change the need to clean visible mold and fix the moisture, so most households skip it.

Locate the leak, then shut off the water to it and dry the wall. If fixing it means plumbing work, the Department of Inspectional Services lists separate plumbing permits, and ordinary repairs and painting are exempt. Ask the department before work starts.
