Water Damaged Hardwood Floors in Melrose, MA: Drying, Cupping and Refinishing

By the Melrose Water Damage Pros team. Published and updated October 29, 2026.

Hardwood absorbs water slowly and releases it slower, which is why a floor that looked fine on Monday can cup by Friday. Fixing water damaged hardwood floors in Melrose, MA comes down to two questions: did the wood dry at a controlled pace, and did the readings settle before anyone sanded?

This guide explains the science in plain terms, including what the equipment does, how moisture readings guide the work, and how to choose between sand-and-refinish and replacement. For the first-hours checklist, a wet floor should start with water mitigation, and the rebuild comes later.

Why hardwood cups

Wood is made of cells that swell when they take on water. When moisture enters from below, the underside of a board swells faster than the top, and the edges lift into a shallow bowl. That is cupping.

The reverse can happen too. When a floor is dried too fast with direct heat, the top shrinks first and the board crowns. Neither shape is a sign the board is ruined, because many cupped floors flatten once the whole board returns to equilibrium.

Where the water usually comes from

The Massachusetts mold guide separates moisture sources into acute events, such as burst pipes, appliance leaks, overflowing tubs and storm flooding, and chronic ones, such as slow leaks in walls or under cabinets, failed caulking and uncontrolled humidity.

Acute events cause fast, visible damage. Chronic ones are sneakier, because a slow dishwasher leak can keep the subfloor damp for months before the boards react. If you see cupping with no obvious spill, look for a chronic source before drying anything.

What the drying equipment does, in plain words

A drying job uses a small group of machines, each with one task.

Air movers

Low, loud fans that push air across the floor surface and lift moisture off the boards.

Dehumidifiers

Pull water vapor out of the room air so the wood has somewhere to release moisture to.

Floor drying mats

Vacuum-style mats that draw moisture through the board face, often used over a plywood subfloor.

Moisture meters

Pin or pinless tools that read how much water is in the wood and the subfloor.

Thermal camera

Shows cold, wet areas on the surface so crews know where to put equipment.

A realistic drying timeline

Numbers vary with the species, thickness, and the amount of water, so treat this as a sequence rather than a schedule.

  1. Day oneExtract standing water, set equipment, and take baseline readings on the boards and subfloor.
  2. Days two through severalReadings are checked daily. Equipment is moved or adjusted, and the dehumidifier is kept running.
  3. StabilizationMoisture should stop dropping and settle near a dry reference reading. Cupping often eases during this stage.
  4. Wait periodBoards sit with equipment removed. Gaps and flatness are rechecked before any sanding decision.

Sand-and-refinish or replace: a decision framework

Condition after dryingUsually refinishUsually replace
CuppingMild and flattening as readings stabilizePermanent warp, boards will not lie flat.
Board thicknessEnough wood above the nails for another sandingWorn thin from earlier refinishing, or engineered veneer.
GapsSmall, even gaps that close seasonallyWide gaps with split edges.
StainingLight surface marksDeep black stains or mold on the back.
Water categoryClean water that was dried quicklySewage or flood water, regardless of appearance.

This is a starting point. A crew with a meter on your actual floor will give a better answer than any chart.

How moisture readings guide each decision

A moisture meter gives a number, but the number only means something next to a reference. Crews take a reading from an unaffected part of the same floor, ideally in a dry room, and use it as the target for the wet area.

Readings are taken on the board face, and on the subfloor where it can be reached. A board that reads dry on top while the plywood beneath it is still wet will cup again, which is why both readings matter.

Ask to see the log. A short table of dates and numbers tells you whether drying is progressing or stalled, and it gives your insurer a clean record of what was done.

What you give up if you sand too soon

Sanding a floor that still holds moisture locks the problem in. The new finish seals the surface while water remains inside, and the cupping often returns within weeks.

Ask for the final readings in writing before anyone starts. Once the floor is dry, sanding, staining and finish coats fall under our water damage repair work, and you can choose the finish sheen without pressure.

Burst pipes and the heating condition

Floors damaged by a frozen pipe raise an insurance question. The state Division of Insurance notes that most homeowners policies cover sudden and accidental water damage from a burst pipe only if the home was properly heated and not left unoccupied.

If you were away in winter, check your policy before you decide who pays for new floors. Read the declarations pages and ask your insurer, and photograph every damaged room in the meantime.

Local note

Melrose notes: older houses and the hardwood underneath

Melrose grew as a commuter suburb after the Boston and Maine Railroad stops were established by 1845, in what are now the Wyoming Hill, Cedar Park and Melrose Highlands areas. That history means many houses are older, and older houses often have wood floors laid over plank subfloors with gaps between boards.

Those gaps let water drop through quickly but also let moisture wick back up for days. If you own one of those houses, tell the crew early about any crawl space or basement beneath the damaged room, because airflow below the floor changes how drying equipment is placed.
