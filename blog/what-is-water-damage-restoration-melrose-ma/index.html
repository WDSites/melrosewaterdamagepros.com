What Is Water Damage Restoration? A Melrose, MA Homeowner Guide

By the Melrose Water Damage Pros team. Published and updated October 29, 2026.

Water damage restoration is the work of stopping a water source, removing standing water, drying the structure, cleaning what got wet, and repairing whatever cannot be saved. In a Melrose, MA house, that might mean a burst supply line in a kitchen wall or a washing machine hose that let go in a second-floor laundry. The published industry standard behind this work is ANSI/IICRC S500.

The short version is that most of the job is drying, not rebuilding. This guide walks through each stage in order, explains the three water categories, and lists questions worth asking any company before it touches your house. If water is still spreading, read the safety notes first and then call our line about emergency water removal.

The six stages of restoration, in order

Every job is different, but the sequence rarely changes. Skipping a stage is how a "fixed" wall ends up with mold behind the paint a month later.

  1. Stop the sourceSomeone has to shut off the water, whether that is the main valve, a fixture stop, or a roof leak that needs a tarp. Restoration crews can control the water they find, but plumbing, roofing and electrical repairs belong to licensed trades.
  2. Assess and map moistureA technician walks the affected rooms with a moisture meter and often a thermal camera. The goal is a map of what is wet, including the wall cavity behind the stain and the subfloor under the tile.
  3. Extract standing waterPumps and extraction units pull out free water. Every gallon removed with a machine is a gallon that does not have to evaporate later, which shortens the whole job.
  4. Dry the structureAir movers push dry air across wet surfaces while dehumidifiers pull moisture out of the room. Crews return to take readings and adjust equipment until the materials reach their normal dry level.
  5. Clean and sanitizeSalvageable contents and surfaces are cleaned, and porous materials that stayed wet or touched contaminated water are removed. Odor control happens here too.
  6. Repair and rebuildOnce everything reads dry, drywall, insulation, flooring, trim and paint go back in. This stage is covered on our water damage repair page.

Water categories and what they mean for your materials

The S500 standard sorts water by how contaminated it is at the source and how much worse it gets over time. The category decides what can be dried in place and what has to come out.

CategoryTypical sourceWhat it usually means
Category 1Broken supply line, overflowing sink with clean water, rainwater from a clean roof leakMost materials can be dried in place if the response is quick.
Category 2Washing machine or dishwasher discharge, toilet overflow with urine only, sump pump failureCarpet padding and porous materials are often removed, hard surfaces are cleaned.
Category 3Sewage backup, river or ground flooding, standing water that sat for daysPorous materials come out, the area is sanitized, and nobody should stay in it.

Clean water can slide to a higher category if it sits long enough, which is why the first day matters.

Why drying takes the most time

Water hides. A ceiling stain the size of a dinner plate can sit on top of insulation that is soaked for several feet in every direction. Drywall, framing and subfloors hold moisture long after the surface feels dry to the hand.

That is why crews measure instead of guessing. Readings are taken on day one and again on return visits, and equipment stays until the materials match a dry reference reading. Pulling machines early saves a day and costs a mold problem, so a good company explains what its numbers say.

Questions to ask any restoration company

Whoever you call, a few plain questions separate a real process from a sales pitch.

  • Do your crews work to the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard, and can you explain how that shapes this job?
  • Will you take moisture readings and share them with me at each visit?
  • How will the scope and price be explained before work starts, and what could change them?
  • Which parts of the job do you subcontract, such as plumbing or electrical?
  • What documentation will I get that I can give my insurer?
  • What does the drying equipment do, how long will it likely run, and who removes it?

What you can do yourself and what to hand off

You can safely shut off the water, move small furniture, lift rugs, and photograph everything. Photos taken before anything is moved help later with an insurance conversation.

The Massachusetts mold guide for homeowners says to hire a professional for plumbing, electrical, structural, roof or siding work, since some of it may legally require a licensed contractor. That applies after a leak as well, so a wet wall with a damaged outlet needs an electrician, not a shop vacuum.

Cleanup of the wet materials themselves is where a restoration company earns its place, because drying a wall cavity without opening it up is guesswork.

Restoration compared with cleanup and repair

People use the three words interchangeably, but they are different stages. Restoration is the umbrella, cleanup is the removal and sanitizing stage, and repair is the rebuild.

If your concern is debris, odor and contaminated materials, water damage cleanup is the closer match. If the house is dry and you are choosing paint and flooring, you are in repair territory.

Local note

Melrose notes: where the water comes from and where responsibility stops

Melrose buys its water from the MWRA, which delivers it from transmission mains into the city system. That system has about 90 miles of pipe and serves more than 8,500 homes, businesses and other facilities. The city is replacing leak-prone pipes and services and runs annual leak detection.

The practical point is where your valve is. City work on mains and services stops at the property line, so a burst pipe inside the house is yours to shut off at the home. Find that main shutoff before you ever need it, and show everyone in the household where it is.
