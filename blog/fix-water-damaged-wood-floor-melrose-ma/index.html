How to Fix a Water Damaged Wood Floor in Melrose, MA

By the Melrose Water Damage Pros team. Published and updated October 29, 2026.

To fix a water damaged wood floor, stop the water, get the surface dry, and decide in the first two days whether boards can stay. In most Melrose, MA homes, the floor you can save is the one you started working on within hours, not the one that sat wet over a weekend.

This is a triage guide, meant to help you act in the first 48 hours. It covers what to do right away, how to tell which floors usually survive, how finish and underlayment change the answer, and when boards have to be replaced. If the water is still coming in, read the safety steps and call about emergency water removal.

Your first 48 hours, hour by hour

The order matters more than speed. Doing step two before step one can put you at risk.

  1. Hour 0: make it safeShut off the water source. If water reached outlets, baseboard heaters or the panel, stay out of the wet area, cut power only if the panel is dry and safe to reach, and call the utility or 911 for any electrical or gas concern.
  2. Hour 1: remove what you canLift area rugs, pull furniture, and take out anything sitting on the floor. Wet metal feet and wet cardboard leave stains that are hard to sand out.
  3. Hour 2: soak up the surfaceMop or wet-vacuum standing water. Do not leave puddles under cabinets, where water wicks into the toe kick and the subfloor.
  4. Hour 4: photograph and move airTake photos of the floor, the water line on walls and any damaged items. Then open windows if the weather allows and run fans across the boards.
  5. Hour 24: check progressThe Massachusetts mold guide for homeowners says wet areas should be completely dry in 24 hours. If the floor still feels cool or damp, it is not on track.
  6. Hour 48: make the callThe state Housing Code expects areas exposed to leaks and floods to be dried within 48 hours. By now you should know whether you need professional drying equipment or board replacement.

Which wood floors usually survive

Solid strip or plank flooring nailed over a plywood subfloor is the most forgiving. It can often be dried in place when the water was clean and the response was fast.

Engineered flooring behaves differently. Its layers can separate at the edges, and a click-lock floating floor may have to be lifted so the underlayment can dry. Laminate, which has a fiberboard core, tends to swell at the seams and rarely recovers fully.

Floors over a concrete slab trap moisture in a different way, because the water has nowhere to go but sideways. That is common in additions and finished basements.

Finish type changes how fast water gets in

A fresh polyurethane finish repels water across the board face but not between the boards, so water finds the seams. An old, worn finish lets moisture straight into the wood.

Look at the edges for crowning, where the board center rises, and at the seams for cupping, where the edges lift. Either shows moisture in the wood, but a moisture meter is the only way to see how much is still inside.

Subfloor and underlayment: the part you cannot see

A floor can look fine while the plywood beneath it keeps absorbing moisture. Foam or paper underlayment under floating floors holds water like a sponge.

If the subfloor stays wet, the new boards you install on top will cup too. Crews check the subfloor with a pin-type meter before anything is laid back down.

For the rebuild side, including replacing boards and matching a stain, see water damage repair.

When boards have to be replaced

Boards are usually replaced when they are warped beyond sanding, when they have separated at the joints, or when mold has grown on the back. Wood that stayed wet through a flood or sewage event is replaced regardless of how it looks.

A single damaged section can often be cut out and patched with new boards woven into the old ones. Stain color and age of the existing floor decide whether the patch shows.

Local note

Melrose notes: permits and when you need one

Melrose permits come from the Department of Inspectional Services, also called the Melrose Building Department. Ordinary repairs are exempt, and so are carpeting, painting and wallpapering. That usually covers swapping a few boards or refinishing a floor.

Permits are required when the repair changes the structure or involves plumbing, electrical, gas or HVAC work. If your water damaged floor came with a burst pipe, the plumbing repair may need its own permit, which goes through the city online portal.

We carry the same approach into nearby towns, including Saugus and Medford, though each municipality has its own rules, so ask the local building department there.
